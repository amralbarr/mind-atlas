# Attitude vs. Skill in Hiring

Between a candidate with strong technical skill but a poor work attitude, and one with a weaker current skill level but excellent attitude, the better long-term hire is usually the latter — skill and knowledge can be taught on the job; punctuality, honesty, and respect for commitments are much harder to instill in an adult employee.

## Why Attitude Is the Harder Constraint

Knowledge gaps close with time — a new hire who doesn't yet know a tool or process will typically know it in a few months. A poor attitude (chronic lateness, disrespect for agreements, no accountability) tends to persist regardless of tenure or training, because it isn't a knowledge problem. Hiring on skill while overlooking clear attitude red flags trades a short-term ramp-up cost for a long-term management burden.

## What "Attitude" Concretely Means

| Signal | What it indicates |
|---|---|
| **Punctuality** | Respect for others' time and for commitments in general |
| **Follow-through on agreements** | Whether verbal commitments can be trusted without constant follow-up |
| **Respect toward more senior/experienced colleagues** | How the person will likely handle feedback and hierarchy under pressure |
| **Ownership of mistakes** | Whether problems get solved or get blamed elsewhere |

## A Rough Hiring Heuristic

> A candidate who is 100% skilled but 80% attitude is a worse hire than one who is 80% skilled but 100% attitude — skill compounds with time on the job; attitude rarely improves without an external reason to change.

This isn't a rejection of skill as a factor — an extreme skill/attitude mismatch in the other direction (poor skill *and* poor attitude) is an easy pass — the heuristic applies specifically to the common tradeoff between "clearly skilled, clearly difficult" and "clearly promising, clearly humble."

## A Real-World Signal: Commute Distance and Commitment

One observed hiring pattern: employees commuting in from outside a big city, who have fewer easy alternative options nearby, often show up more reliably and on time than local candidates for whom switching jobs or working remotely from a cafe is a low-friction, always-available option. This isn't a rule to prefer one candidate pool over another — it's an illustration that ease of alternatives can quietly erode commitment, and that a candidate's circumstances (not just their stated intentions) are worth factoring into an honest read of likely reliability.

## Correcting Attitude Issues Directly, Early

When a manageable attitude issue shows up (a punctuality problem, a communication gap with a manager), the effective response is a direct, private conversation addressing it specifically — not public correction, and not silent tolerance. Publicly embarrassing someone for a mistake (even a real one) tends to produce resentment rather than correction; privately naming the specific behavior and the expected standard tends to actually change it.

## Common Questions

**Doesn't hiring for attitude over skill risk lowering the technical bar of a team?**
> Not if there's a reasonable skill floor already required — the heuristic is for close calls between two viable candidates, not a license to hire someone with no relevant competence at all. Skill floor and attitude filter are both necessary; they answer different questions.

**How does this interact with career development advice for the employee side?**
> The same principle applies in reverse — an employee's fastest path to being trusted with more responsibility is usually demonstrated reliability and attitude, not accelerated technical learning alone; see [Career Progression Before Entrepreneurship](career-progression-before-entrepreneurship.md).
