# Opportunity Evaluation Framework

Not every business opportunity should be judged by the same criterion (immediate payment). Depending on career/business stage, the right criterion for accepting an opportunity might be portfolio value, market credibility, or relationship equity instead of cash — but this only works as a *deliberate strategy*, not an excuse to always work for free.

## Criteria for Evaluating an Opportunity

| Criterion | When it's the right lens |
|---|---|
| **Cash value** | Default lens once there's an established track record and portfolio — take the opportunity that pays best for the effort |
| **Portfolio/case-study value** | Early stage, entering a new vertical, or lacking proof in a specific category — the goal is evidence to unlock future higher-value work |
| **Brand-name value** | A recognizable client name, even at reduced pay, that can be referenced to win future higher-value clients ("I've worked with X") |
| **Relationship/timing value** | An opportunity that may not pay immediately but positions for a larger one later (a "harvest" that comes due after the relationship matures) |

## A Real Example: Working for Free, Deliberately

Early in a marketing career, taking on a first client for free — with the explicit goal of proving results within a trial period before any paid contract — is a form of investing effort instead of capital in building a portfolio. In one documented case, a single free engagement (an eyelid-surgery clinic, run through a month-long unpaid trial with a full [customer journey funnel](../../../marketing/concepts/fundamentals/customer-journey.md) built from scratch) led not only to a paid retainer, but to being referred into an entire medical center's business — years later, still an active client. The free work was a deliberate bet on proof-of-concept and referral value, not an ongoing habit of underpricing.

## The "1+1=3" Framing

Some opportunities are explicitly *investments* rather than transactions — accepting below-market terms once, deliberately, in exchange for a name, a category entry, or a portfolio piece that's worth more than the cash difference over time. This only works if it's a calculated, occasional trade — treating every deal this way (or being talked into it repeatedly by clients who exploit the framing) erodes both income and market positioning; see the anti-pattern warning in [Client Selection Criteria](client-selection-criteria.md).

## Timing: Recognizing "Harvest" Moments

Some opportunities aren't really about the immediate deal — they're a payoff moment for prior positioning (a strong portfolio, a personal brand, a track record) finally converting into a large contract or opportunity. Recognizing when an opportunity is this kind of "harvest," versus another investment-stage opportunity, changes how it should be evaluated and negotiated.

## Common Questions

**How many "investment" opportunities should someone take before switching fully to cash-value evaluation?**
> There's no fixed number — the signal to switch is having enough proof (case studies, referenceable names, referral flow) that the portfolio/credibility gap the investment opportunities were meant to close is actually closed. Continuing to accept below-market deals past that point is usually just underpricing, not strategy.

**Isn't this just a justification for being taken advantage of?**
> It becomes that the moment it's not a deliberate, bounded choice — the distinction is intent and scarcity: a rare, chosen investment in a specific opportunity for a specific strategic reason, versus a recurring pattern driven by an inability to say no (see [Client Selection Criteria](client-selection-criteria.md)).
