# Client Selection Criteria

A service provider or agency should evaluate a prospective client the same way the client evaluates them — being selective about *who* to take on is a legitimate, necessary business practice, not arrogance, especially once demand exceeds capacity.

## Signals a Client Is a Bad Fit

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Rushed urgency** | A client who needs results "immediately" often hasn't budgeted realistic time for strategy to work, and will judge the relationship against an unrealistic timeline |
| **Micromanagement** | Signals the client won't trust professional judgment, which erodes the ability to do good work and burns disproportionate account-management time |
| **Category mismatch (ethical/personal red lines)** | A provider should have clear categories they won't work in, decided in advance — not improvised case-by-case |
| **Scale mismatch** | A client whose expected volume/scope is larger than the provider's current team can properly service — better to decline honestly than to accept and deliver a degraded experience that damages reputation |
| **History of "shopping" many providers with no results** | A client who describes having burned through many prior providers is sometimes signaling unrealistic expectations rather than consistently bad luck with providers — worth probing before accepting |

## Interview the Client, Without Making It Feel Like an Interview

The evaluation should happen naturally within the sales conversation — asking about their prior experience with providers, their internal team, their expectations and timeline — rather than as a formal, separate vetting step that feels adversarial. The goal is information, not confrontation.

## Capacity-Aware Selectivity

Being selective isn't just about difficult personalities — it's also math. A service business has a real ceiling on how many clients its current team can properly serve at high quality (e.g. an account manager responsible for several accounts at once, each requiring real attention). Taking on more than that ceiling to avoid saying no to revenue degrades service for *every* existing client, not just the new one — the cost of over-acceptance is invisible until quality drops across the board.

## Turning Down a Client Gracefully

Declining a client, whether due to capacity or fit, should still preserve the relationship for the future — "we're not able to take this on right now" (a scheduling/capacity statement) preserves the door for a referral back later, and is honest, versus quietly under-delivering to a client that should have been declined up front.

## Common Questions

**Should a new/early-stage provider be this selective?**
> Selectivity should scale with demand — an early-stage provider with abundant capacity and few clients has less room to turn work away; selectivity becomes viable (and necessary) once demand consistently outpaces capacity, particularly once a personal or company brand is generating consistent inbound interest (see [Personal Branding](../../../marketing/concepts/branding/personal-branding.md)).

**How does client selection connect to setting expectations?**
> Good selection reduces (but doesn't eliminate) the need for expectation management — a well-matched client still needs a clear roadmap and realistic KPIs; see [Client Expectation Management](client-expectation-management.md).
