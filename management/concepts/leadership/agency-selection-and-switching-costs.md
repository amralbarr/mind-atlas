# Agency Selection & Switching Costs

Choosing a marketing agency (or any specialist vendor) purely on upfront price tends to cost more in the long run than committing to the right, more expensive option from the start — because every switch between vendors resets progress and repeats onboarding cost.

## The Hidden Cost of Hopping Between Cheap Vendors

A common pattern: a business tries a small, inexpensive agency, sees underwhelming results after a few months, switches to another small agency, repeats the disappointment, and switches again — accumulating a total spend across several failed attempts that often exceeds what one competent, appropriately-priced agency would have cost from the outset. Each switch also costs *time* (a new agency's learning curve, re-explaining the business and brief — see [Client Briefing & Discovery](../client-relationships/client-briefing-and-discovery.md)) that a single sustained relationship wouldn't have lost.

## Why the Cheaper Option Often Isn't Cheaper

A lower quote can reflect genuinely lower cost structure and efficiency — or it can reflect less experience, weaker talent, and a higher chance of an expensive relearning cycle. Since the true cost of a bad agency choice includes the switching cost described above, evaluating "cheap vs. expensive" purely on the quoted fee, without weighing the probability of having to switch again, systematically undercounts the real cost of the cheaper option.

## What This Doesn't Mean

This isn't an argument to always pick the most expensive available option — it's an argument to evaluate an agency on demonstrated track record and fit (see [Client Selection Criteria](../client-relationships/client-selection-criteria.md) for the mirror-image version of this same evaluation, from the agency's side of choosing clients) rather than defaulting to the cheapest quote as a cost-saving shortcut.

## A Related Signal: Being Chosen as "The Model to Copy"

A strong indicator that an agency (or any service business) has built real credibility: when a prospective client explicitly says they want their business run *like* one of the agency's existing accounts. That kind of reference-driven inbound interest reflects earned reputation (see [Personal Branding](../../../marketing/concepts/branding/personal-branding.md)) and tends to correlate with a track record worth paying a premium for.

## Common Questions

**How can a business tell in advance if an agency is worth the higher price?**
> Concrete case studies and references from comparable clients, not just a confident pitch — asking to speak with an existing client in a similar category is a reasonable, common due-diligence step before committing.

**Does this same logic apply to hiring an in-house marketing manager instead of using an agency?**
> Yes — see [In-House Marketing Team vs. Agency](in-house-marketing-team-vs-agency.md) for the equivalent risk of hiring cheaply (or hiring based on interview charisma alone) and having to redo the hire later.
