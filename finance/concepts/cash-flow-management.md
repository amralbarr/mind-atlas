# Cash Flow Management

A business can be profitable on paper and still fail because cash isn't available *when* obligations (salaries, rent, supplier payments) come due. Cash flow management is about timing — when money actually arrives and leaves — not just whether revenue exceeds cost over a period.

## Don't Put All Revenue in One Basket

Relying on a single payment structure across the whole business (e.g. every sale on 60-90 day credit terms) creates a fragile cash position — a temporary slowdown in *any one* channel can leave the business without cash for weeks even while paper revenue looks fine. Diversifying between immediate-cash channels and credit/deferred-payment channels reduces this fragility.

## The Real Cost of Credit Terms ("Net Terms")

Extending payment terms to a customer or distributor isn't free — it has a real, quantifiable cost equivalent to interest, often underestimated:

> A month of deferred payment can cost roughly 1.5-3% of the deal's value (varying with the prevailing cost of capital/inflation environment) — over a 3-month term, that can consume a large share of the deal's entire profit margin.

A deal that looks like a healthy 20% margin can have 60-70%+ of that margin quietly consumed by a 3-month payment term, once the real cost of that delay is calculated — turning what looked like a good deal into a marginal one. This should be priced into any deal that includes deferred payment, not treated as a free accommodation to the customer.

## Cash Flow Is Getting Harder in Long Supply Chains

In multi-step supply chains (raw material supplier → manufacturer → retailer/reseller), each link has traditionally extended credit terms to the next. As economic conditions tighten, terms compress at every link (a supplier who used to offer 90 days now offers 30), and that compression cascades — a manufacturer squeezed by a shorter-paying supplier will, rationally, try to shorten the terms it extends downstream too. A business anywhere in this chain needs to actively manage which terms it can still safely extend, rather than assuming historical terms will hold.

## Moving Toward Cash-First Channels

**Online/direct-to-consumer sales are effectively pure cash** — payment happens at or near the point of sale, with no extended credit period. A business historically dependent on wholesale/retail credit terms can deliberately shift more of its volume toward direct online channels specifically to *improve cash flow*, independent of whether the online channel is more profitable per unit — the cash-timing benefit is a distinct reason to shift channel mix, not just a margin argument.

## A Transitional Model: Retail Presence as Trust-Building, Not Volume

A business that has proven itself online doesn't necessarily need to move volume *back* to physical retail to grow — a small number of physical locations can serve a trust-building function (a place customers know exists, can visit if something goes wrong) while the bulk of transaction volume and cash flow stays in the faster-cash online channel.

## Common Questions

**If online is pure cash and wholesale isn't, why would any business still use wholesale/credit terms?**
> Wholesale/distribution channels can reach volume and market segments a direct online channel can't reach alone (institutional buyers, bulk purchasers, geographic reach), so the trade-off is between cash-flow safety and market reach — the right mix depends on the category, not a universal rule to abandon credit terms entirely.

**How does this connect to break-even calculations?**
> [Break-even and unit economics](break-even-and-unit-economics.md) tells you whether a deal is profitable on paper; cash flow timing (including the real cost of any credit term) tells you whether that profit is actually collectible when your own bills are due — a deal can pass the first test and still create a cash crisis if the second is ignored.
