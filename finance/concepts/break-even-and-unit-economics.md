# Break-Even & Unit Economics

Knowing the true cost of one unit sold — not just its material/production cost — is the foundation of correct pricing. Many businesses that look profitable on paper are actually losing money because their unit cost ignores overhead and seasonality.

## Fixed Cost vs. Variable Cost

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed cost (overhead / "head cost")** | Doesn't change with sales volume | Rent, salaries, fixed utilities |
| **Variable cost** | Scales directly with each unit produced/sold | Raw materials, packaging, per-unit shipping |

A price that only covers variable cost plus a margin — while ignoring fixed cost entirely — silently loses money on every unit once fixed costs are actually paid at the end of the month, even while the business "feels" profitable unit by unit.

## Allocating Fixed Cost Per Unit

The correct method: take the *full annual* fixed cost, divide it by *expected annual unit sales*, and add that per-unit overhead burden on top of the direct unit cost before pricing.

> **Per-unit overhead = Total Annual Fixed Cost ÷ Expected Annual Unit Sales**
>
> **True Unit Cost = Direct Unit Cost + Per-Unit Overhead**

Treating inventory sitting in a warehouse as "free" until sold is a common mistake — it's still accruing storage, insurance, and opportunity cost exactly like an idle employee, and should be priced accordingly.

## Accounting for Seasonality ("Down Season")

Many businesses have a "high season" (strong sales) and a "down season" (weak sales), but *fixed costs don't pause* during the down season — rent and salaries are still due. A break-even calculation that only looks at strong-season sales volume overstates the business's real margin, because it implicitly assumes every month sells like the best month. The fix: calculate expected annual unit sales *including* the weak months, not by extrapolating from the strong season alone.

## Break-Even Point

> **Break-Even Units = Total Fixed Cost ÷ (Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit)**

Every unit sold below the break-even volume is a net loss for the period; every unit above it contributes to profit. A business that doesn't know this number is, by definition, pricing and planning blind — it can't tell whether a given month's sales volume was actually profitable or just busy.

## Why This Prevents Bad Pricing Decisions

A business owner who doesn't know their true unit cost is vulnerable to two symmetric mistakes: undercutting a competitor's price without knowing if it's even profitable, or accusing a competitor of price-gouging when the competitor may simply have priced correctly for costs the accuser didn't account for in their own business. Both mistakes trace back to the same root cause — not having done this calculation.

## Common Questions

**Why would two competitors selling a similar product at very different prices both be profitable?**
> Different cost structures, different volume assumptions, or different [pricing strategies](../../marketing/concepts/fundamentals/pricing-strategies.md) (e.g. one pursuing market-penetration pricing deliberately) can make both prices individually rational — price alone, without knowing the other side's cost structure, doesn't tell you who's "right."

**How does this connect to cash flow?**
> Break-even is about profitability *on paper* over a period; [cash flow management](cash-flow-management.md) is about whether the money is actually available *when* it's needed — a business can be break-even-profitable and still run out of cash if receivables are collected too slowly relative to when its own bills are due.
