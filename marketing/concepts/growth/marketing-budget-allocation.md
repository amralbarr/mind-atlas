# Marketing Budget Allocation

A common early-stage mistake is launching a business having spent every available unit of capital on the product itself, leaving nothing meaningful for marketing — then treating a token amount (a small fixed sum per month) as "doing marketing." A more reliable rule is to plan marketing spend as a **percentage of total launch capital**, scaled to the size of the business.

## A Rule-of-Thumb Scale

| Business capital | Suggested marketing investment | Why the ratio shifts |
|---|---|---|
| ~1,000,000 | ~300,000-500,000 | A new, unknown entrant needs disproportionate spend just to become visible against established competitors |
| ~5,000,000 | ~2,000,000 | Still front-loaded, because market share has to be *taken* from incumbents, not just requested |
| ~10,000,000+ | ~3,000,000+ | The ratio can taper somewhat as brand recognition starts compounding |

These are directional, not universal fixed percentages — the exact ratio depends on category competitiveness, whether the business is new or already has some brand recognition, and margin structure. The point of the table is the *shape* of the decision: marketing at a new business's launch is not a minor line item, it's often the single largest lever determining whether the business is ever discovered at all.

## Marketing Investment Is Not Just Media Spend

A meaningful marketing budget covers more than ad spend: content production, [influencer/creator fees](../digital-marketing/content-creator-types.md), PR, and — increasingly — audience-building work that produces no *direct* sale but builds the reputation and content library that later campaigns depend on. Treating "marketing budget" as synonymous with "ad spend" is what leads a business to underfund production and then wonder why the ads underperform (see [MER](../analytics/marketing-efficiency-ratio-mer.md), which explicitly captures this broader cost).

## The Payback Framing

Marketing spent in the early months of a launch is better modeled as an investment with a delayed return than as a monthly expense — a heavy first-quarter spend to establish a market position (visibility, initial customer base, case studies) is expected to convert into a lower ongoing acquisition cost in subsequent months, as brand recognition, retargeting pools, and word-of-mouth start doing some of the work organically. Judging month-one ROI in isolation, without this framing, leads to premature budget cuts right before the investment would have paid off.

## Common Questions

**Isn't it safer to start with a small marketing budget and scale up only once something proves to work?**
> A very small budget often can't produce a fair test — a token daily spend usually isn't enough for a platform's algorithm to find the right audience efficiently, so a "failed" cheap test may just have been underfunded rather than genuinely unpromising. See [Creative Testing & Scaling](../digital-marketing/creative-testing-scaling.md) for how testing budgets should be sized to actually produce a signal.

**How does this interact with pricing?**
> The marketing budget and the product's [pricing strategy](../fundamentals/pricing-strategies.md) have to be planned together — underpricing while overspending on acquisition, or vice versa, breaks the unit economics regardless of how good either decision looks in isolation.
