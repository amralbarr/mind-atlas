# Growth Hacking Basics

An approach to growth that prioritizes fast, cheap, data-driven experimentation over large, slow, traditional marketing campaigns — most associated with early-stage startups that don't have big budgets but can move quickly and tolerate failed experiments.

## Growth Hacking vs. Traditional Marketing

| | Traditional Marketing | Growth Hacking |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Often large, upfront | Often minimal, reinvested as it proves out |
| Timeline | Campaigns planned months out | Rapid experiments, days to weeks |
| Channels | Established (TV, print, big-platform ads) | Anything that works, including unconventional or one-off tactics |
| Mindset | Brand-building, broad reach | Metric-driven, funnel-stage-specific |

Growth hacking isn't a replacement for brand or fundamentals — it's a fast-iteration mode of finding what works before committing bigger, slower budget to it.

## The Growth Hacking Loop

1. **Analyze** — find the biggest bottleneck in the [funnel](marketing-funnel-aida.md) (often via the AARRR stages).
2. **Ideate** — generate many possible experiments to address that specific bottleneck.
3. **Prioritize** — score ideas by expected impact, confidence, and effort (a common framework is **ICE**: Impact, Confidence, Ease).
4. **Test** — run the highest-priority experiments, usually via [A/B testing](../analytics/ab-testing.md) or a fast, low-cost launch.
5. **Repeat** — kill what doesn't work fast, double down on what does.

## Classic Examples (for pattern recognition, not to copy blindly)

- **Built-in virality** — a product feature that inherently exposes new users to it (e.g. "Sent via [Product]" signatures, referral-gated features).
- **Platform leverage** — using a larger platform's existing audience as a distribution channel before the platform closes the loophole (early social integrations are the canonical example).
- **Scarcity/exclusivity** — invite-only launches that create demand and word-of-mouth before public availability.

These worked in specific market and platform conditions — the lesson is the *pattern* (find an underused distribution lever), not that any single tactic will still work today.

## Common Questions

**Is growth hacking sustainable long-term?**
> Individual hacks often have a short shelf life (platforms close loopholes, novelty fades), which is why mature growth teams treat it as a continuous experimentation process rather than a one-time trick — the discipline of testing and measuring is the durable part, not any specific tactic.

**How does growth hacking relate to product?**
> Many effective growth loops are built into the product itself (referral mechanics, viral sharing, activation flow), which is why growth work increasingly sits close to product teams rather than purely within marketing.
