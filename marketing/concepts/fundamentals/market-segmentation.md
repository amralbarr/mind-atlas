# Market Segmentation

Dividing a broad market into smaller groups of consumers who share similar characteristics, needs, or behaviors — so marketing can be targeted rather than generic.

## The Four Main Bases

| Type | Splits customers by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Demographic** | Age, gender, income, education, occupation | "Working professionals aged 25-40" |
| **Geographic** | Location, climate, urban vs. rural | "Customers in GCC countries" |
| **Psychographic** | Values, lifestyle, personality, interests | "Environmentally conscious consumers" |
| **Behavioral** | Purchase habits, brand loyalty, usage rate, benefits sought | "Frequent buyers who respond to loyalty discounts" |

## Why Segment

- **Relevance**: a message tailored to a segment outperforms a one-size-fits-all message.
- **Efficient spend**: budget goes toward the segments most likely to convert, not everyone.
- **Product decisions**: segments often reveal which features or price points actually matter to real buyer groups.

## Segmentation → Targeting → Positioning (STP)

Segmentation is step one of a three-step process:

1. **Segment** the market into meaningful groups.
2. **Target** the segment(s) worth pursuing (evaluate size, growth, fit, competition).
3. **Position** the product in the minds of that target segment — see [Positioning](positioning.md).

## Common Questions

**What makes a segment "viable"?**
> It should be measurable (you can size it), substantial (large enough to be worth targeting), accessible (you can reach it through some channel), and differentiable (it responds differently to marketing than other segments).

**How does segmentation relate to buyer personas?**
> A segment is a data-driven group; a [buyer persona](buyer-personas.md) is a semi-fictional, humanized representation of a typical member of that segment, used to make marketing decisions more concrete.
