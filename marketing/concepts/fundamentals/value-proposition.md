# Value Proposition

A clear statement of the tangible benefit a customer gets from choosing a product — why it's better or different from the alternatives, including doing nothing. It answers: *"Why should I buy this, from you, instead of anything else?"*

## Structure

A strong value proposition usually names three things:

1. **The target customer** — who this is for.
2. **The core benefit** — the outcome they get, not the feature that produces it.
3. **The differentiator** — why this is better than the alternative (competitors, or the status quo).

## Feature vs. Benefit vs. Value Proposition

| Level | Example |
|---|---|
| **Feature** | "Automatic nightly backups" |
| **Benefit** | "Never lose your work" |
| **Value proposition** | "The only backup tool built for freelancers who can't afford an IT team — automatic, invisible, and restores in one click" |

A value proposition is built from benefits, but it's sharpened by a specific audience and a specific comparison — a benefit alone is generic; a value proposition is a claim about being the best choice *for someone, against something*.

## A Simple Template

> For **[target customer]** who **[has this need/problem]**, **[product]** is a **[category]** that **[key benefit]**. Unlike **[main alternative]**, we **[key differentiator]**.

## Common Questions

**How is a value proposition different from a tagline?**
> A tagline is the short, memorable public-facing phrase; the value proposition is the underlying strategic statement that the tagline, landing page, and sales pitch are all derived from. A tagline is not required to state the value proposition explicitly — the landing page copy usually does that.

**Where does the value proposition show up in marketing?**
> It should be the first thing communicated on a landing page (usually the headline + subheadline), and it should be traceable through ad copy, sales scripts, and onboarding — inconsistency here is a common cause of confused, low-converting marketing.
