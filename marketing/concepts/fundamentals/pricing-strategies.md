# Pricing Strategies

Price is one of the [4Ps](marketing-mix-4ps.md), but it's also the single most common cause of business failure — badly-priced businesses can have great sales volume and still be losing money every month. Setting a price is a strategic choice, not "competitor's price times a multiplier."

## Core Pricing Strategies

| Strategy | Logic | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** | Cost of the item + a fixed margin | Simple, low-risk, but ignores what the market will actually bear |
| **Value-based** | Price set by the value delivered (expertise, outcome, brand), not cost | Services, premium products, anyone with strong differentiation or reputation |
| **Market-penetration** | Deliberately low price to win market share fast, accepting thin or negative margin early | New entrants competing against established players, or building a portfolio/reputation |
| **Premium / prestige** | High price used *as a signal* of quality | Categories where price and perceived quality are strongly linked in the buyer's mind |

A business or freelancer often moves through these over time: penetration pricing to break in and build proof, then shifting to value-based pricing once reputation and results exist to justify it.

## Why Cost-Plus Alone Fails

Cost-plus pricing that only accounts for the direct cost of one unit (materials, production) while ignoring **fixed/overhead costs** (rent, salaries, seasonal down-periods) systematically underprices the business. The fix is to allocate the full **head cost** across expected annual unit sales — see [Break-Even & Unit Economics](../../../finance/concepts/break-even-and-unit-economics.md) for the full method. A business that prices only against material cost can look profitable all year and still lose money once overhead and slow-season months are accounted for.

## Price as a Quality Signal

In categories where buyers can't easily verify quality themselves (furniture, fashion, cosmetics), price becomes a proxy for quality — a middle-class-and-up buyer generally won't believe a much cheaper product is equally good, regardless of the seller's claims. This means underpricing relative to the category's expectations can *hurt* sales by triggering suspicion ("if it's this cheap, something must be wrong with it"), not just leave money on the table. Justifying a genuinely lower price at the same quality requires proof — branding, content, and social proof that build enough trust to override the price-quality assumption; see [Personal Branding](../branding/personal-branding.md).

## The "Downgrade" / Trading-Down Trend

During economic pressure, consumers don't necessarily stop buying a category — they change *how* they buy within it: smaller quantities bought more frequently (a $5 item bought four times instead of one $20 item), or switching from an established international brand to a comparable local/generic alternative. This shift shows up across categories (fashion, cosmetics, manufacturing sourcing) and is a structural shift in buying behavior, not simply "less demand" — a pricing and packaging strategy that only offers large, high-commitment purchase sizes can miss an entire trading-down segment that would still buy, just smaller and more often.

## Common Questions

**How do I know if my price is actually covering my real costs?**
> Divide your full annual fixed/overhead cost by your expected annual unit sales to get the per-unit overhead burden, then add that on top of direct unit cost *before* adding margin — see [Break-Even & Unit Economics](../../../finance/concepts/break-even-and-unit-economics.md).

**Is matching a competitor's lower price always a good move?**
> Not automatically — a competitor pricing lower might have a fundamentally different cost structure (or might be underpricing by mistake, ignoring their own overhead). Racing to match a price without knowing your own break-even point is one of the most common ways businesses erode margin without realizing it.
