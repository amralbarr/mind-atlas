# Brand Voice & Messaging

**Brand voice** is the consistent personality a brand's writing has across every piece of communication — the verbal counterpart to a visual identity. **Messaging** is the specific set of claims and language a brand uses to communicate its value, built on top of that voice.

## Voice vs. Tone

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Consistent, doesn't change | A brand that is always "direct and no-nonsense" |
| **Tone** | Adapts to context | Direct and no-nonsense, but *warmer* in a support email about a billing error, *sharper* in a competitive ad |

Voice is the personality; tone is that personality adjusted for the moment — the same person is more careful breaking bad news than making a joke, without becoming a different person.

## Defining a Voice

A simple way to pin down a voice is a set of "we are / we are not" pairs:

> We are **direct**, not vague. We are **confident**, not arrogant. We are **plain-spoken**, not jargon-heavy.

This is more actionable for a writing team than adjectives alone, because it rules out the nearby trap (confident sliding into arrogant).

## Messaging Hierarchy

A messaging framework typically has layers, from strategic to tactical:

1. **Value proposition** — the core claim (see [Value Proposition](../fundamentals/value-proposition.md))
2. **Key messages** — 3-4 supporting pillars (e.g. speed, reliability, price)
3. **Proof points** — data, testimonials, or features backing each pillar
4. **Copy** — the actual sentences used in ads, landing pages, and sales material, derived from the layers above

Keeping this hierarchy explicit is what keeps a large team's copy consistent without every writer inventing new claims independently.

## Common Questions

**Why does brand voice matter for conversion, not just brand feel?**
> Inconsistent or generic voice reads as untrustworthy or forgettable, which quietly hurts conversion even when the offer itself is strong — people buy from brands that feel coherent and confident.

**How is messaging different from a tagline?**
> A tagline is one short, public output of the messaging framework; messaging itself is the full internal set of claims and proof points that every piece of copy — tagline included — should trace back to.
