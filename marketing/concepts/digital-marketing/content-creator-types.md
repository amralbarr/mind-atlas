# Content Creator Types & Message Source Credibility

Who *appears* to be speaking in a piece of content changes how much an audience trusts it — this is **message source credibility**, a core idea from communication theory that explains why the same claim lands differently depending on who says it.

## The Two Levers of Source Credibility

| Lever | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trustworthiness** | The source is an authority or has a track record | A celebrity, an established expert, a well-known founder |
| **Similarity** | The audience sees themselves in the source | "Someone like me" using the product |

Trustworthiness borrows credibility from status; similarity borrows credibility from relatability. Neither is universally better — the right one depends on the category and the claim being made.

## Content Creator Types

| Type | Stands for | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **FGC** | Founder-Generated Content | Building trust in the company itself — audiences increasingly want to see the person behind the brand, not just the product |
| **EGC** | Employee-Generated Content | Authentic, behind-the-scenes brand storytelling — often outperforms polished brand content because it reads as unscripted (an employee's off-hand video can outperform a paid campaign) |
| **UGC** | User-Generated Content | Genuine product reviews from real customers — the "similarity" lever, since a real customer looks like the next customer |
| **IGC** | Influencer-Generated Content | Borrowed trust and reach from an established audience — the "trustworthiness" lever |
| **CGC** | Creator-Generated Content | UGC-*style* content produced by a paid creator rather than an actual customer — increasingly common as "authentic-looking" content becomes its own production category, distinct from a genuine unpaid review |

## Matching the Creator to the Segment

The single biggest mistake in creator selection isn't picking someone unpopular — it's picking someone who doesn't match the target audience or the product's positioning. A creator who visibly signals a *premium* lifestyle promoting a *budget* product (or vice versa) breaks source credibility even with a large following: the audience buying the budget product doesn't see themselves in a luxury lifestyle, and the mismatch reads as inauthentic. Selecting creators — like selecting guests for a podcast, or casting a persona for an ad — should be filtered on "does this face/voice look like our actual buyer," not follower count alone.

## Why This Matters for Content Volume

Different creator types compound: a single well-performing organic FGC or EGC clip can outperform months of paid production, but it can't be manufactured on demand. A practical content strategy pairs a few high-trust anchor pieces (FGC/EGC) with an ongoing stream of UGC/CGC across many creative angles — see [Creative Testing & Scaling](creative-testing-scaling.md) for how that volume gets produced and tested systematically.

## Common Questions

**Why does an unscripted employee video sometimes outperform a professional ad?**
> Because it maximizes the *similarity* lever — the employee looks and sounds like a real person, not a performer, so the audience's guard drops. Professional polish can actually work against similarity-based credibility, even though it strengthens trustworthiness-based credibility.

**Should influencer content always be "put through" paid ads, or is organic reach enough?**
> Both — organic influencer content builds reach and credibility passively, but running the strongest-performing pieces as paid ads (with the right audience targeting) usually multiplies their return, since the platform can now show the content to people beyond the influencer's existing following.
