# Marketing KPIs & Metrics

Key Performance Indicators — the small set of metrics a team actually tracks to judge whether marketing is working. The core skill is picking metrics that connect to business outcomes, not just ones that are easy to measure.

## Core Acquisition Metrics

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **CAC** (Customer Acquisition Cost) | Total acquisition spend ÷ new customers | How much it costs to win one customer |
| **LTV** (Customer Lifetime Value) | Avg. revenue per customer × avg. customer lifespan | How much a customer is worth over time |
| **LTV:CAC ratio** | LTV ÷ CAC | Whether acquisition spend is sustainable (3:1 is a common healthy benchmark) |
| **Conversion rate** | Conversions ÷ visitors (or leads) | How effectively traffic turns into action |
| **CPL** (Cost Per Lead) | Spend ÷ leads generated | Efficiency of lead-generation campaigns |

## Vanity Metrics vs. Actionable Metrics

| Vanity | Actionable |
|---|---|
| Followers, impressions, pageviews | Conversion rate, CAC, LTV, retention rate |

A vanity metric can go up while the business doesn't improve at all (10,000 impressions to an irrelevant audience). An actionable metric changes a decision — if CAC rises above LTV, that's a signal to stop or fix a channel, not just a number to report.

## Leading vs. Lagging Indicators

- **Leading indicators** move first and predict future results — email signups, trial starts, content engagement.
- **Lagging indicators** confirm the outcome after the fact — revenue, churn, LTV.

Tracking only lagging indicators means problems are visible only after they've already cost money; leading indicators give time to react.

## North Star Metric

A single metric a whole team rallies around because it best captures the value being delivered to customers (e.g. "weekly active users" for a social app, "bookings made" for a marketplace). Marketing KPIs should ultimately connect to this metric, not exist in isolation.

## Diagnosing a Rising CAC: A Real Example

A furniture brand's marketing manager watched CAC roughly double period over period — from a healthy figure to nearly double that. Rather than treating the number alone as the problem, the diagnosis worked backward through the funnel: the cost of the underlying message/lead unit had itself roughly doubled, which meant the *number* of messages received for the same budget had roughly halved — and since the conversion rate of message-to-sale had stayed the same, half as many messages produced half as many sales at the same cost, doubling CAC. The follow-up questions that matter once CAC moves like this: did a new competitor enter and bid up the same audience, does the audience/targeting need to be refreshed, do the current offers and promotions need updating, or has the creative simply stopped keeping up with current trends? A rising CAC is a symptom — the useful work is tracing it back to *which* upstream number actually moved.

## Common Questions

**Why do LTV:CAC calculations often mislead early-stage companies?**
> LTV is hard to estimate accurately without enough retention history, and early adopters often behave differently (more loyal, more forgiving) than the broader market the business will eventually acquire — an early LTV:CAC ratio can look better than what happens at scale.

**What's the difference between CAC and CPL?**
> CPL measures cost per lead (someone who showed interest); CAC measures cost per *paying customer*. A channel can have a low CPL but a high CAC if its leads convert to paying customers poorly.
