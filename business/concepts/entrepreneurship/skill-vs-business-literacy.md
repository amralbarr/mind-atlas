# Skill vs. Business Literacy

Being excellent at a craft — cooking, design, marketing execution, a trade — does not transfer automatically into being able to run a business built on that craft. This gap is one of the most common, least discussed causes of new-business failure.

## Why the Gap Exists

A skilled practitioner's expertise is in *producing the output* (a great dish, a great campaign, a great garment). Running a business built on that output requires a completely different skill set: pricing, cash flow, operations, hiring, and strategic planning — none of which the craft itself teaches. A brilliant chef opening a restaurant, or a skilled marketer starting a marketing agency, both fail at roughly the same rate for the same underlying reason: technical excellence was never business literacy.

## The Pattern Across Consulting Engagements

Across many different businesses and countries, the single most common root cause behind a struggling business — more than the specific industry, product, or team — is that the owner does not understand fundamental business mechanics: pricing, financial management, operational planning. This holds even for founders who are demonstrably excellent at their craft, which is what makes the failure mode counterintuitive: expertise is not the missing ingredient, business literacy is.

## Study Business Before Converting a Skill Into a Business

The practical recommendation is not "get an MBA before doing anything" — that bar is too high and too slow for most people. It's narrower: read broadly across the core business functions (strategy, sales, marketing, finance) *before* committing capital to a venture, so that when the time comes to hire specialists or negotiate with agencies/vendors, there's enough literacy to evaluate their advice rather than being led by whoever sounds most confident.

## The Two Paths When the Gap Exists

If a skilled practitioner doesn't want to (or can't yet) close the business-literacy gap themselves, there are two honest paths:

1. **Study first, then launch** — invest time in learning strategic planning, pricing, and financial basics before starting, even informally.
2. **Partner instead of solo-founding** — bring in a business-literate co-founder or partner and become the technical/craft side of the partnership; see [Technical/Business Co-Founder Partnership](technical-business-cofounder-partnership.md).

What doesn't work reliably is launching alone on skill alone and learning business fundamentals reactively, after money is already at risk.

## Common Questions

**Does this mean skilled people shouldn't start businesses?**
> No — it means the craft skill should be paired with either self-taught business literacy or a partner who has it, before scaling. Plenty of craft experts build successful businesses; the failures cluster specifically among those who assumed the craft alone was sufficient.

**What's the minimum literacy needed before starting, if a full business education isn't realistic?**
> At minimum: understanding [break-even and unit economics](../../../finance/concepts/break-even-and-unit-economics.md), a basic [pricing strategy](../../../marketing/concepts/fundamentals/pricing-strategies.md), and enough of [cash flow management](../../../finance/concepts/cash-flow-management.md) to know that profit on paper isn't the same as money in the bank.
